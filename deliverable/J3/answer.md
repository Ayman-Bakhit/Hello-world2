# J3: AI voice outbound calls for small dental clinics (US federal rules)

Research date: 2026-11-24. Planned launch: January 2027. Federal law only. Product-scoping analysis, not legal advice.

## 1. Decision

**GO-WITH-CONSTRAINTS for call types A (reminders/rescheduling) and B (recall). NO-GO for C (promotional whitening calls) as planned. C can only ship as an opt-in module that collects and stores the patient's prior express written consent itself.**

Confidence:
- A: medium-high. The consent basis is clear, provided the patient gave the number to the clinic.
- B: medium. Recall calls fit the literal definition of "telemarketing". The health-care carve-outs probably cover them, but that point is not settled.
- C as planned: high that it is a no-go. An AI-voice ad call needs signed written consent, and "existing patient" or "filled out a web form" is not that consent.

Self-serve onboarding with no lawyer review per clinic is workable only if the compliance rules are built into the code (blocked fields, call caps, scripts the clinic cannot edit, consent records). It does not work if each clinic decides for itself what is legal. I still recommend one review of the product design by TCPA counsel before launch. That is a single review of the product, not a review per clinic, so it stays within your constraint.

Why this matters financially: the TCPA's private right of action pays $500 per violating call, and up to $1,500 if willful ([47 U.S.C. 227(b)(3)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2023-title47/html/USCODE-2023-title47-chap5-subchapII-partI-sec227.htm)). Volume makes small error rates expensive (math in section 5).

## 3. Federal rule map by call type

Key legal facts (Verified, S1/S2/S5):
- An AI-generated voice counts as an "artificial voice".
- To a **cell number**, any non-emergency artificial-voice call needs **prior express consent** (PEC).
- If the call is telemarketing or an advertisement, it needs **prior express written consent** (PEWC). The exception is a "health care message" made by or for a HIPAA covered entity or business associate, which needs only PEC (64.1200(a)(2)).
- To a **residential landline**, telemarketing needs PEWC (64.1200(a)(3)). A health-care message needs no consent if calls stay within 1 per day and 3 per week and opt-outs are honored (64.1200(a)(3)(v)).

Do not rely on the "free-to-end-user" wireless healthcare exemption in 64.1200(a)(9)(iv). It requires calls that are not charged to the patient or counted against plan limits. It also limits content to listed purposes (appointment reminders, wellness checkups and similar) with no telemarketing. I could not confirm that a small startup can make calls meet the free-to-end-user condition (unverified). Use PEC as the legal basis and copy (a)(9)(iv)'s safeguards anyway (caps, 1-minute calls, immediate opt-out). That costs little and protects you.

## 4. Product constraints (built into the code, not clinic options)

1. **Purpose lock.** Ship only fixed call purposes: confirm, remind, reschedule, recall-due. The AI agent must not mention prices, specials, new services or financing, and must not do billing or collections. Enforce this through script templates, an output filter, and refusals inside the conversation. One unscripted upsell can turn a PEC call into a telemarketing call that needs PEWC (64.1200(a)(2)).
2. **Per-number consent record.** The upload must include, for each number: (a) the patient gave this number to the clinic, (b) the date, and (c) the source (intake form or portal). Reject or quarantine numbers that were appended, bought, or added by family or third parties, unless the clinic records the patient's own consent (S7 ¶142 context). The clinic attests in the terms of service, but the product still enforces the fields.
3. **Reassigned-number check.** Query the FCC Reassigned Numbers Database using the consent date before a number's first call and again on a recurring schedule. Suppress any number reported as disconnected. This is the only federal safe harbor for calls to reassigned numbers (64.1200(m)). Old dental patient lists make this a top risk.
4. **Opening disclosure on every call.** Within the first seconds: the clinic's **legally registered business name**, not just a brand name ((b)(1)), and a callback number ((b)(2)). I also recommend a short "this is an automated assistant" line. That is not federally required yet (S6 is only proposed), but it is cheap insurance if S6 is finalized.
5. **Automated opt-out on every call**, announced right after identification (within 2 seconds where (b)(3) applies; do it on all calls for simplicity). It should work by voice or keypress, record the opt-out instantly, and end the call. Voicemails include a toll-free number that connects to the same automated opt-out. Name this as the **exclusive revocation method** and disclose it on each call (S3, effective Nov. 25, 2026). Process opt-outs immediately; the legal maximum is 10 business days and S4 may shorten it.
6. **Revocation scope.** An opt-out on a reminder or recall call stops that category for that clinic. That is allowed (S3), but blocking all calls from that clinic is the safer default. An opt-out from any promo call stops **all** promo calls from that clinic (S3).
7. **Frequency caps for every patient:** at most 1 call per day and 3 calls per week, each about a minute or less. This meets (a)(3)(v) for landlines without needing to detect the line type.
8. **Calling window:** 8 a.m. to 9 p.m. in the patient's local time ((c)(1)), using the patient's address rather than area code where available.
9. **Company DNC system** per clinic: an auto-generated written DNC policy, opt-out records kept 5 years, and no sharing of opt-outs without consent ((d)(1)-(6)).
10. **Promo module (C) off by default.** It is unlocked only for patients with a PEWC record captured by the product: an e-signed web or tablet consent naming the clinic, the (f)(9) disclosures, and a timestamp, IP address and text version stored. Promo calls also get a National DNC scrub (registry version no more than 31 days old, (c)(2)(i)(D)) and the abandonment and ring-time rules ((a)(6)-(7)). Texting is not a workaround: the same consent rules apply to texts ([89 FR 5098](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/01/26/2023-28832/targeting-and-eliminating-unlawful-text-messages-implementation-of-the-telephone-consumer-protection)).
11. **HIPAA business associate agreement** with every clinic before upload. The health-care-message carve-outs only cover calls "made by, or on behalf of, a covered entity or its business associate" (S2 (a)(2), (a)(3)(v)). HIPAA security obligations are a separate workstream I did not research.
12. **Caller ID:** show only a number the clinic owns or has authorized, never a misleading one (227(e)).
13. **Records** of consent evidence, scripts and transcripts, RND results and opt-outs. Keep them at least 4 years. That figure assumes the TCPA statute of limitations is 4 years under 28 U.S.C. 1658, which I did not verify.
14. **Platform-liability guardrails.** The platform can be treated as the caller if it is "so involved" in the calls or knowingly allows unlawful use (S7 ¶30). Do not let clinics edit scripts freely or switch off the consent and opt-out gates. Suspend accounts after complaint spikes. Indemnity in your contract helps but does not remove the risk.

## 5. Exposure math (scenario inputs are assumptions)

Script: `exposure.py` (run in session). Damages are $500 per call, or $1,500 if willful (S1 227(b)(3)). The volume inputs are made-up scenarios, not market data.

| Scenario (assumed: 50 clinics x 1,500 patients x 6 calls/yr = 450,000 calls) | Bad calls | Base exposure | Treble |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1% defective (reassigned / revoked / no consent) | 450 | $225,000 | $675,000 |
| 0.5% defective | 2,250 | $1,125,000 | $3,375,000 |
| 2.0% defective | 9,000 | $4,500,000 | $13,500,000 |
| One clinic, one 2,000-patient whitening blast without PEWC | 2,000 | $1,000,000 | $3,000,000 |

The point: an early-stage SaaS business cannot survive even a 0.5% defect rate if a class action is filed. This is why constraints 2, 3, 5 and 10 must be enforced by the product rather than left to clinics.

## 6. What would flip the decision

- **Recall treated as telemarketing.** If a court rules that recall calls are telemarketing and not "health care messages" (more likely now that courts can disagree with FCC readings, S8), B needs PEWC and moves toward the C path. Fallback: run B as SMS or human calls, or collect PEWC at intake.
- **AI-specific consent rule.** If the FCC finalizes S6 with a requirement that consent mention AI, existing PEC may not cover AI calls and clinics would need to re-consent patients. That would be a major hit to onboarding. Watch the AI proceeding (CG Docket 23-362, docket number from memory; S6 lists it).
- **Consent provenance.** If clinics cannot show that patients gave their numbers directly (merged lists, PMS imports with no source field), those records cannot be called. If that is most records, A and B stop working.
- **Free-to-end-user path.** A confirmed, practical way to meet that condition would allow a lighter (a)(9)(iv) route for wireless reminders. That would ease the analysis but not change the go decision.
- State laws (for example state "mini-TCPA" and telemarketing statutes) can be stricter. I flag them only; I did not research them.

## 7. Alternatives considered

- **Do nothing:** no risk and no revenue.
- **SMS-only reminders:** the same TCPA consent rules apply, but the AI-voice question goes away and opt-out (STOP) is simpler.
- **Reminders only (A) at launch, B after a counsel opinion, C last:** recommended sequencing.
- **Human-staffed calls for promos:** not "artificial voice", so (a)(2)-(3) do not apply, but DNC and calling-hours rules still do (live-call consent rules not researched in depth).
