How Much Does Credentialing Cost in 2026?

Category: Credentialing

    Posted By: Noah Stone

    Posted Date: Oct 21, 2026

    Payers charge most physicians and other practitioners nothing to credential in 2026. Medicare's $750 application fee applies only to institutional providers, such as hospitals and DME suppliers. For a practice asking how much does credentialing cost, the bill is the work: credentialing companies publish rates of $100 to $500 per payer application.

    Price every quote by the application. One provider joining one payer counts as one application, so a physician who joins six payers files six applications. Multiply your providers by your payers, and you have the count each line of a quote should tie back to.

    Four things move the total: provider type, the number of payers you join, specialty extras such as DEA registration or a CLIA certificate, and who does the work. An individual practitioner and a hospital face different fees, and staff, software, or a credentialing company will bill the same applications in different ways.

    Key takeaways

    • Medicare: $0 for physicians, NPPs, and their groups; $750 per application for institutional providers in 2026.
    • Commercial payers: Aetna and Cigna list no application fee, and CAQH (now called DataSpring) is free for providers.
    • Credentialing companies: $100 to $500 per payer application on the pricing pages we checked in October 2026.
    • MedSole RCM: $99 per payer application, charged only after the payer approves it, with revalidation and recredentialing included and no monthly fee.
    • Delays: each week a provider waits for approval is a week of visits you can't bill that payer for as in-network.

    What Credentialing Fees Cover

    Credentialing fees are every charge tied to getting a provider approved to bill a payer: government fees, platform fees, and the cost of the people doing the work. Keep three terms apart. Credentialing is the payer verifying the provider, enrollment sets up billing, and contracting sets the rates.

    • Government and payer fees: application, registration, and certificate fees, $0 for most individual practitioners.
    • Platform fees: the CAQH (DataSpring) profile costs providers nothing, while credentialing software runs on a paid subscription.
    • Service fees: a company's per-application price or your own staff's hours, which make up most of the bill.

    Most answers to how much does credentialing cost blend these buckets into one number. If you've typed "how much does it cost to get credentialed with insurance companies" into Google, the answer you need sits in the third bucket.

    How Much Does Credentialing Cost With Medicare?

    Physicians, NPPs, and their group practices pay $0 to enroll in Medicare. Institutional providers pay the 2026 Medicare enrollment application fee of $750, which CMS resets each year using the CPI-U. The 2025 fee was $730.

    Under the PECOS fee rules, institutional providers pay that fee when they file an initial enrollment, revalidate, or add a practice location. A hospital or agency facing real financial strain can ask CMS for a hardship exception with its application.

    For a practitioner, the cost to get credentialed with Medicare is the paperwork and the wait. Our Medicare enrollment guide walks through PECOS step by step. CMS hadn't published the CY2027 fee as of October 19, 2026; it released the 2026 notice on December 3, 2025.

    Is CAQH Free in 2026?

    Yes. CAQH rebranded as DataSpring in June 2026, the provider portal works the same way, and the DataSpring clinician FAQ says providers won't be charged to use it. Any CAQH cost you see on a quote pays a person to keep your profile current.

    You'll still spend time on it. Many payers require you to re-attest the profile on a set cycle, and Aetna and Cigna both pull credentialing data from it. Our CAQH credentialing guide covers setup and attestation in order.

    How Much Do Credentialing Companies Charge?

    Credentialing companies charge $100 to $500 per payer application, based on the prices and ranges we found on eight credentialing and billing company pricing pages in October 2026. Most of what people mean by how much does credentialing cost is this line: the credentialing cost per payer, multiplied by every payer and every provider.

    Vendors label that line in different ways. One quote calls it a payer enrollment cost, another lists an insurance credentialing services cost per plan, and a third folds it into a package. Convert each quote to a price per application before you compare them side by side.

    How Do I Calculate Credentialing Costs?

    Calculate credentialing in two parts: what you pay to get approved, and what you can't bill while you wait. The second part is where credentialing revenue impacts show up, and for a busy provider it's the bigger number by a wide margin.

    Fees = providers × payers × price per application, plus government fees (institutional providers only), plus DEA or CLIA if needed, plus any monthly fee × months.

    Delay exposure = visits per day with that payer × average allowed amount per visit × working days the application is pending.

    Take a provider who would see eight patients a day with one commercial payer, at an average allowed amount of $110 a visit. Approval takes 20 working days. Those inputs are examples, so swap in your own numbers from your practice management system.

    The math runs 8 × $110 × 20 = $17,600 in visits you can't bill that payer as in-network until it approves the application. Add the fee formula to the delay formula, and you have your full credentialing cost for that provider and payer.

     

    Backlogs are common. In a November 2025 MGMA Stat poll, 32% of 131 responding practices said their credentialing work was behind. That figure combines backlogs (23%), CVO delays (3%), and slips into the next quarter (6%).

    What Happens When a Provider Isn't Credentialed Yet?

    The provider can still see patients, but claims to that payer won't pay as in-network until the payer approves the application. Medicare follows a written rule for the start date, while commercial payers write their own.

    Under 42 CFR 424.520, Medicare sets the effective date as the later of two dates. One is the day the provider filed an application that Medicare later approved; the other is the day the provider first furnished services at the new location.

    Physicians and NPPs can bill Medicare for up to 30 days before that date if circumstances kept them from enrolling sooner, under 42 CFR 424.521. For commercial payers, check the payer's retroactive billing policy before you put a new provider on the schedule.

    Applications slow down for predictable reasons. The payer verifies each license, degree, and work-history item, credentialing committees meet on fixed schedules, and an incomplete application or an un-attested CAQH profile sends the file back to you.

    Credentialing gaps show up first as denials in your AR. MedSole works both sides: credentialing at $99 per approved payer application and outsourced medical billing services at 2.99% of collections. Our team catches a stalled application before the claims pile up.

    How Much Does Credentialing Cost in California, Texas, and New York?

    In all three states, individual physicians and NPPs pay no Medicaid application fee. Institutional providers pay $750 in 2026, and states often waive it when the provider already paid the fee to Medicare or another state's Medicaid program.

    The federal rule, 42 CFR 455.460, requires states to collect the fee from fee-liable provider types and excludes individual physicians and NPPs. For a practitioner, the cost to get credentialed with Medicaid is time and paperwork.

    How to Lower Credentialing Costs Without Slowing Approval

    Most of the cost of credentialing with insurance companies is time and rework, so you save money by keeping applications from stalling. These six habits cut the most waste:

    1. Credential only with the payers your patients use; pull 12 months of payer mix before you pick panels.
    2. Get each quote priced per payer application, and ask whether you're charged at submission or after approval.
    3. Keep the CAQH (DataSpring) profile complete and attested, since an expired attestation can stall an application.
    4. Start applications as soon as a new provider's start date is set, because each week of delay is a week you can't bill that payer.
    5. Put each renewal date in one calendar: re-attestation, three-year recredentialing, five-year Medicare revalidation, DEA, and CLIA.
    6. Choose pricing that includes revalidation and recredentialing, so the next cycle doesn't come with a second bill.

    Credentialing Cost FAQs

    How Much Does Credentialing Cost per Month?

    For most practices, credentialing costs $0 a month. The CAQH (DataSpring) portal is free, and payers don't bill a monthly fee. A monthly cost appears only when a vendor charges for maintenance, with one published example at $45 to $60 per provider a month, or when you buy software. MedSole charges no monthly fee.

    Who Pays for Credentialing, the Provider or the Practice?

    In most cases, the practice pays when it credentials a provider under its group contracts, and independent providers pay their own. Check the employment agreement before you assume either way. Some contracts shift the cost to the provider or let the practice recover it if the provider leaves early.

    What's the Cheapest Way to Get Credentialed With Insurance Companies?

    Filing on your own costs $0 in payer fees for individual practitioners, and you pay in staff time and the risk of a stalled application. If you outsource, two numbers decide the cost: the per-application price and whether the company bills before or after approval.     Do You Have to Pay a Company to Get Credentialed?
