How Much Does Credentialing Cost in 2026? Category: Credentialing Table of Contents ▼ Posted By: Noah Stone Posted Date: Oct 21, 2026 Payers charge most physicians and other practitioners nothing to credential in 2026. Medicare's $750 application fee applies only to institutional providers, such as hospitals and DME suppliers. For a practice asking how much does credentialing cost, the bill is the work: credentialing companies publish rates of $100 to $500 per payer application. Price every quote by the application. One provider joining one payer counts as one application, so a physician who joins six payers files six applications. Multiply your providers by your payers, and you have the count each line of a quote should tie back to. Four things move the total: provider type, the number of payers you join, specialty extras such as DEA registration or a CLIA certificate, and who does the work. An individual practitioner and a hospital face different fees, and staff, software, or a credentialing company will bill the same applications in different ways. Commercial payers: Aetna and Cigna list no application fee, and CAQH (now called DataSpring) is free for providers. Keep three terms apart. Credentialing is the payer verifying the provider, enrollment sets up billing, and contracting sets the rates. Government and payer fees: application, registration, and certificate fees, $0 for most individual practitioners. Platform fees: the CAQH (DataSpring) profile costs providers nothing, while credentialing software runs on a paid subscription. Physicians, NPPs, and their group practices pay $0 to enroll in Medicare. Institutional providers pay the 2026 Medicare enrollment application fee of $750, which CMS resets each year using the CPI-U. The 2025 fee was $730. Under the PECOS fee rules , institutional providers pay that fee when they file an initial enrollment, revalidate, or add a practice location. A hospital or agency facing real financial strain can ask CMS for a hardship exception with its application. For a practitioner, the cost to get credentialed with Medicare is the paperwork and the wait. Our Medicare enrollment guide walks through PECOS step by step. CMS hadn't published the CY2027 fee as of October 19, 2026; it released the 2026 notice on December 3, 2025. Is CAQH Free in 2026? Yes. CAQH rebranded as DataSpring in June 2026, the provider portal works the same way, and the DataSpring clinician FAQ says providers won't be charged to use it. Any CAQH cost you see on a quote pays a person to keep your profile current. You'll still spend time on it. Many payers require you to re-attest the profile on a set cycle, and Aetna and Cigna both pull credentialing data from it. Our CAQH credentialing guide covers setup and attestation in order. How Do I Calculate Credentialing Costs? Calculate credentialing in two parts: what you pay to get approved, and what you can't bill while you wait. The second part is where credentialing revenue impacts show up, and for a busy provider it's the bigger number by a wide margin. Fees = providers × payers × price per application, plus government fees (institutional providers only), plus DEA or CLIA if needed, plus any monthly fee × months. Delay exposure = visits per day with that payer × average allowed amount per visit × working days the application is pending. Take a provider who would see eight patients a day with one commercial payer, at an average allowed amount of $110 a visit. Approval takes 20 working days. Those inputs are examples, so swap in your own numbers from your practice management system. The math runs 8 × $110 × 20 = $17,600 in visits you can't bill that payer as in-network until it approves the application. Add the fee formula to the delay formula, and you have your full credentialing cost for that provider and payer. Backlogs are common. In a November 2025 MGMA Stat poll , 32% of 131 responding practices said their credentialing work was behind. That figure combines backlogs (23%), CVO delays (3%), and slips into the next quarter (6%). What Happens When a Provider Isn't Credentialed Yet? The provider can still see patients, but claims to that payer won't pay as in-network until the payer approves the application. Medicare follows a written rule for the start date, while commercial payers write their own. Under 42 CFR 424.520 , Medicare sets the effective date as the later of two dates. One is the day the provider filed an application that Medicare later approved; the other is the day the provider first furnished services at the new location. Physicians and NPPs can bill Medicare for up to 30 days before that date if circumstances kept them from enrolling sooner, under 42 CFR 424.521 . For commercial payers, check the payer's retroactive billing policy before you put a new provider on the schedule. Applications slow down for predictable reasons. The payer verifies each license, degree, and work-history item, credentialing committees meet on fixed schedules, and an incomplete application or an un-attested CAQH profile sends the file back to you.
